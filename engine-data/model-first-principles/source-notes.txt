Break a problem down to what is certainly true, then rebuild.
What it is: Instead of copying how things are usually done, strip a problem back to the facts you know are true and build your answer up from there.
A simple example: Everyone assumed battery packs had to be expensive. One team priced the raw materials (cobalt, nickel, carbon, aluminium) and found they cost a fraction of the pack price. The real cost was in how packs were made, not what they were made of.
When to use it: When "that is how it is always done" is the only reason given, when you are stuck, or when costs and rules seem fixed.
A common mistake: Calling an assumption a fact. Test each "fact" by asking how you know it is true.
Ask yourself: What do I know for certain, and what am I only assuming because everyone else does?

More notes:
Origin: Aristotle described a first principle as the first basis from which a thing is known. Modern popular example, widely reported: in a 2012 interview Elon Musk described battery packs costing around 600 dollars per kilowatt hour, then asked what the raw materials (cobalt, nickel, aluminium, carbon, polymers, a steel can) would cost on the metals market, and found it was around 80 dollars per kilowatt hour; the gap was in how packs were made. Contrast with reasoning by analogy: doing things because that is how they have always been done or how others do them. Method: 1) state the problem and the current assumptions, 2) break it down to what you know is definitely true, 3) rebuild a solution from those facts. Close with a question the listener can apply to a problem of their own this week.
