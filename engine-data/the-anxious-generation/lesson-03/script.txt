=== Two Worlds ===
[two_worlds] Picture a child who is not allowed to walk to the corner shop alone.
Yet that same child can wander the entire internet from bed, unsupervised.
=== Fear of the Outdoors ===
[tv_news] In the 1980s and 1990s, frightening news stories about abducted children spread fear among American parents.
[milk_carton] Faces of missing children even appeared on milk cartons.
[crime_chart] Yet Haidt points out that crime in the United States was actually falling from the early 1990s.
=== Kids Pulled Indoors ===
[parent_hand] Parents responded by keeping children close and constantly supervised.
[school_walk] Walking to school, playing in the park, or running errands alone became rare.
[safety_label] Haidt calls this mindset safetyism, where almost any risk is treated as unacceptable.
=== The Open Door Online ===
[smartphone] Meanwhile, a brand new world arrived in children's pockets.
[strangers] On smartphones, kids could meet strangers, see violent or sexual content, and be targeted by companies.
=== An Age With No Gate ===
[age_signs] In the real world, we set minimum ages for driving, drinking, and signing contracts.
[age_13] Online, the key age became 13, set by an American privacy law from 1998.
But companies rarely checked anyone's age.
[checkbox] A ten year old could simply tick a box claiming to be old enough.
=== Not Built for Kids ===
[mars] Haidt offers a thought experiment.
Would you let a company send your child to grow up on Mars, with no testing of how children would fare?
[ad_machine] Social media was not built for children either.
It was designed to capture attention and sell ads, not to protect young minds.
=== Upside Down ===
[seesaw] So protection ended up upside down.
[scraped_knee] Kids were shielded from scraped knees and the small adventures that help them grow.
[shield] Yet online, where serious dangers were piling up, they were left almost alone.
Haidt sums it up simply: too much protection in the real world, too little in the virtual one.
=== Rebalance ===
[balance] His fix is to rebalance.
Give kids more freedom offline and put more limits online.
[kid_outside] Let them take small real-world risks, and delay their entry into the online world.
=== Your Turn ===
[question] Think of a child in your life.
Where could you loosen your grip outdoors, and where should you tighten it on their screens?
