=== Getting Things Done ===
[team_table] Great teams don't just have good ideas, they turn them into results.
Kim Scott says the secret engine is the candid relationships you have already built.
=== The Wheel ===
[wheel] Scott describes a cycle she calls the Get Stuff Done wheel.
[seven_steps] It has seven steps: listen, clarify, debate, decide, persuade, execute, and learn.
Then the wheel turns again.
[trust_hub] Candor is the hub that keeps every spoke strong.
=== Listen ===
[ear] It starts with listening, so that ideas can surface from anyone, not just the boss.
[steve_jobs] Scott contrasts Steve Jobs, who would state strong opinions and invite people to fight back.
[tim_cook] Tim Cook listened quietly, asking question after question until he truly understood.
Both styles work, as long as people feel safe speaking up.
=== Clarify ===
[seedling] New ideas are fragile, like seedlings.
[workspace] So give people time and space to sharpen an idea before it faces tough scrutiny.
Then help them explain it clearly enough that others can understand it.
=== Debate and Decide ===
[debate_ring] Next, debate the idea hard, focusing on the issue, not on egos.
[scale] Then decide.
[decider] Scott urges pushing decisions to the people closest to the facts, rather than having the boss rule on everything.
=== Persuade ===
[megaphone] A decision means little if the wider team doesn't buy in.
[head_heart] Scott suggests appealing to logic, to emotions, and to your own credibility.
[nodding_crowd] Done well, people commit rather than merely comply.
=== Execute ===
[calendar] Then comes execution, the actual work.
[shield] Scott says a good boss protects the team's time, cutting needless meetings so people can focus.
And stay close enough to the work to spot problems early.
=== Learn ===
[magnifier] Finally, look back at what worked and what didn't.
Were you right or wrong?
[loop_arrow] Admit it openly, then start the wheel again, a little wiser.
Without candor, this step turns into blame instead of learning.
=== Your Turn ===
[question] Think about your team's last big decision.
Which step of the wheel did you rush or skip, and how could a candid conversation fix it next time?
