=== Title ===
[ticker_tape] Most people buy a stock like a lottery ticket flashing on a screen.
Graham says you are actually buying a slice of a real company.
=== A Share Is a Shop ===
[corner_shop] Picture a bakery on your street, with ovens, staff, and loyal customers.
[share_slice] Owning one share means owning a small slice of that bakery.
So the real question is simple: would you want to own this business?
=== Think Like an Owner ===
[graham] Graham wrote that "investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike."
[checklist] For careful investors, he turned that idea into a short checklist.
Let us walk through it, one test at a time.
=== Big Enough ===
[size_bar] First, size.
Graham wanted large, established companies, not small firms that one bad year could sink.
In his day, he suggested at least 100 million dollars in yearly sales for an industrial company.
[inflation_up] Today, that bar would be far higher.
=== Strong Finances ===
[scales] Second, financial strength.
Short-term assets, like cash and inventory, should be worth at least twice the bills due within a year.
[debt_weight] Long-term debt should be smaller than what is left after paying those bills.
=== Steady Profits ===
[timeline] Third, steady earnings.
The company should have made a profit in each of the past 10 years.
[growth_arrow] Graham also wanted earnings per share to grow by at least a third over that decade.
=== Reliable Dividends ===
[coins] Fourth, dividends.
Look for a company that has paid shareholders without a break for at least 20 years.
[calendar] An unbroken record shows a business that keeps delivering through good times and bad.
=== A Fair Price ===
[price_tag] Finally, price.
Even a wonderful bakery is a bad buy if you pay far too much.
[pe_meter] Graham suggested paying no more than 15 times average earnings over the last 3 years.
[book_value] He also preferred a price below one and a half times the net assets on its books.
=== Your Turn ===
[magnify] Pick one stock you own or want, and run it through these five tests.
If it fails, ask yourself honestly: am I buying a business, or just a ticker?
