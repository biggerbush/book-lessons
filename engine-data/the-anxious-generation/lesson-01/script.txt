=== A Sudden Turn ===
[teen_window] Around 2012, something strange began happening to teenagers in many countries at once.
Their mental health started falling, fast.
=== The Hockey Stick ===
[line_chart] Picture a chart of teen depression that runs flat for years.
[sharp_bend] Then, in the early 2010s, the line suddenly bends upward like a hockey stick.
Jonathan Haidt reports that rates of depression among American teens more than doubled within about a decade.
[er_door] Anxiety rose too, and so did hospital visits for self-harm, especially among younger girls.
=== Not Just America ===
[world_map] This was not one country's problem.
[flag_pins] Haidt finds similar upward lines in Britain, Canada, Australia, and the Nordic nations.
Kids who lived thousands of miles apart were struggling in the same years.
So the cause had to be something they all shared.
=== What Changed? ===
[question_mark] Haidt asks a simple question: what changed in kids' lives right around then?
[calendar] His answer points to the years between 2010 and 2015.
That is when teens moved their social lives onto smartphones.
=== Flip to Smart ===
[flip_phone] In the late 2000s, most teens carried basic flip phones.
They used them to call or text friends, then put them away.
[smartphone] A smartphone was different.
It brought the whole internet, endless apps, and social media into every pocket, all day long.
=== Selfies and Feeds ===
[front_camera] Around 2010, phones gained front-facing cameras, making selfies easy.
[photo_feed] Image-sharing apps like Instagram took off, and teens began posting and comparing themselves constantly.
=== Ruling Out Rivals ===
[money_chart] Could it be the 2008 financial crisis instead?
Haidt points out that teen mental health kept worsening even as the economy recovered.
[globe_spin] And no single country's events could explain such a sudden, shared, worldwide jump.
=== The Great Rewiring ===
[playground] Haidt calls this shift the Great Rewiring of childhood.
[kids_on_couch] In just a few years, a play-based childhood became a phone-based childhood.
Children spent less time together in person and far more time alone, staring at screens.
[test_tube] He sees it as a giant experiment run on kids, without anyone asking first.

[two_photos] Think about when you got your first smartphone, and how old you were.
Then ask: what did a normal afternoon look like before, and after?
