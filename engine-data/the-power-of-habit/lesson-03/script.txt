=== Smells Like Success ===
[spray_bottle] Picture a spray that wipes out any bad smell, and nobody wants to buy it.
That puzzle reveals the real engine behind every habit.
=== A Product That Flopped ===
[pg_lab] Procter and Gamble created a clear spray that could remove odors from fabric, called Febreze.
[smelly_couch] Early ads promised to kill smells, like cigarette smoke or a wet dog on the couch.
[falling_chart] Sales were dismal.
=== The Nose Goes Blind ===
[cat_house] Researchers visited a woman whose home was full of cats.
[shrug_woman] The smell was strong, but she shrugged it off, because she simply could not smell it anymore.
[blind_nose] When we live with an odor every day, our noses tune it out.
So the people who most needed Febreze never felt the cue to use it.
=== A Finishing Touch ===
[clean_room] Then researchers watched a woman who used it differently.
[made_bed] After vacuuming and making the bed, she gave the room a few happy sprays.
[sparkle] For her, Febreze was not about bad smells, but a little celebration of a finished job.
=== The Relaunch ===
[perfume_drop] The company added more perfume so each spray smelled clearly fresh.
[new_ad] New ads showed people spraying freshly cleaned rooms, with the windows open to fresh air.
[rising_chart] Sales took off, and Febreze became a huge success.
=== Wanting Before Getting ===
[monkey] Duhigg describes a lab monkey that learned to pull a lever when a shape appeared on a screen.
[juice_drop] Each time, it got a drop of juice.
[brain_spark] Over time, its brain lit up the moment the shape appeared, before any juice arrived.
That anticipation is craving, and it is what keeps a habit running.
=== Build Your Own Craving ===
[sneakers] So if you want a new habit to stick, pair it with a reward you can look forward to.
[treat] After a run, enjoy a small treat or savor feeling proud.
[anticipation] Soon your brain will expect that good feeling, and craving will pull you out the door.
