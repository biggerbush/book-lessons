=== The Prize That Hurts ===
[golden_carrot] What if offering someone a prize could make them slower at solving a problem?
Daniel Pink says it happens more often than we think.
=== Two Kinds of Work ===
[checklist] Some tasks are algorithmic, meaning you follow a set path to one answer.
[assembly_line] Think of adding up invoices or stacking boxes on a line.
[maze_map] Other tasks are heuristic, meaning there is no script and you must invent a solution.
=== The Candle Puzzle ===
[candle_table] Picture a table holding a candle, a box of thumbtacks, and some matches.
[cork_wall] Your job is to fix the candle to a wall so the wax will not drip on the table.
Most people try tacking the candle straight on, or melting it to stick.
=== The Hidden Answer ===
[empty_box] The trick is to empty the tack box and pin it to the wall.
[candle_shelf] The box becomes a little shelf, and the candle sits on top.
The hard part is seeing the box as more than a container.
=== Adding a Prize ===
[scientist] Psychologist Sam Glucksberg gave this puzzle to two groups.
[stopwatch] He told the first group he was simply timing them to see how long it usually takes.
[cash_stack] He offered the second group cash if they were among the fastest solvers.
=== The Slow Winners ===
[bar_chart] The group chasing money took about three and a half minutes longer, on average.
The reward was supposed to sharpen them.
[blinkers] Instead, it narrowed their focus, so they stared at the obvious and missed the clever answer.
=== Flip the Puzzle ===
[loose_tacks] Glucksberg then ran a version with the tacks dumped beside the empty box.
Now the solution was easy to spot, and the task became routine.
[finish_flag] This time, the group offered cash finished faster.
=== What It Means ===
[if_then_sign] Pink's lesson is that if-then rewards, do this and get that, suit simple work.
[lightbulb] For creative work, they can hold people back.
[gift_box] So skip the advance promise, and if you want to reward great creative work, do it as a surprise afterwards.
